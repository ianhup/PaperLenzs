## Title
Laboratory cooking and degradation experiments to support interpretations of absorbed lipid residues arising from the processing of broomcorn millet (Panicum miliaceum) and animal products in archaeological pottery vessels I. Molecular analysis

## Topics
Archaeological chemistry; organic residues; millet; pottery; lipids

## Introduction
Archaeologists need chemical markers to identify ancient plant processing despite mixing and long-term degradation in pottery.

## Methods
Replica vessels are used to cook millet alone and millet with animal products, followed by lipid extraction and degradation experiments.

## Results
Meat fats increase incorporation of the millet marker miliacin; miliacin is relatively degradation-resistant, whereas many other lipid compounds decline.

## Discussion
Mixed residue signatures require careful interpretation because processing conditions change which chemical markers survive.

## Conclusion
Miliacin alongside animal-fat markers can help infer ancient co-cooking practices.

## Relevance to research
Not relevant: archaeological food-residue chemistry is unrelated to modern Arctic tern prey abundance, bird migration timing, wind conditions, or global warming impacts.

## Title
Why Most Published Research Findings Are False

## Topics
Research reliability; statistical power; prior odds; bias; multiple testing; positive predictive value

## Introduction
The essay examines why apparently significant research claims may fail to replicate and why a single p-value cannot determine whether a proposed relationship is true.

## Methods
A mathematical framework relates the positive predictive value of significant findings to pre-study odds, statistical power, and Type I error, then considers bias and competition among research teams. Illustrative scenarios explore how these factors change credibility.

## Results
Under unfavorable combinations of low prior odds, low power, multiple testing, and bias, a large proportion of significant findings can be false. The conclusion is conditional on model assumptions, not a direct census showing that a fixed percentage of all published papers is wrong.

## Discussion
The argument emphasizes study design, effect size, transparency, and cumulative evidence over significance alone. It also notes the value of negative findings and the risks of selective reporting.

## Conclusion
Improving reliability requires stronger designs, less bias, and interpretation of findings in the context of prior plausibility and replication.

## Relevance to research
Methodologically relevant to evaluating the mockup. It supports caution about multiple species and interaction analyses, modest site counts, and interpreting p = 0.17 as insufficient evidence of a richness difference rather than proof of equality.

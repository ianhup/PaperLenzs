## Title
Do social frontiers matter for depression?

## Topics
Residential segregation; social boundaries; depression; spatial regression; neighborhood epidemiology

## Introduction
Abrupt ethnic or religious boundaries between neighborhoods may affect mental health in ways not captured by conventional measures of segregation or group density.

## Methods
The authors linked English small-area depression diagnoses and antidepressant prescriptions with ethnic and religious frontier estimates from the 2011 and 2021 censuses. Spatial binomial and Poisson regressions incorporated covariates, overdispersion, and a spatially lagged response term.

## Results
Frontier intensity for several ethnic and religious groups was consistently associated with neighborhood depression prevalence. For Chinese/non-Chinese frontiers in 2021, a five-percentage-point intensity increase corresponded to an estimated 1.5-2.2% higher depression rate.

## Discussion
Area-level associations cannot establish individual mechanisms or causality. Administrative outcomes, confounding, and spatial dependence require careful interpretation, and estimates vary across groups and outcomes.

## Conclusion
Community boundaries warrant attention when investigating relationships between residential segregation and mental health.

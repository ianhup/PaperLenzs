## Title
Biomass harvesting intensity influences above and below ground wood decomposition processes in a northern Ontario jack pine forest

## Topics
Forest management; deadwood; decomposition; nutrient cycling; habitat; biomass removal

## Introduction
Deadwood decomposition supports habitat, nutrient cycling, and carbon storage. The study investigates whether increasingly intensive biomass removal changes decay and chemical dynamics relative to natural or recovering reference conditions.

## Methods
A replicated Ontario jack-pine harvest trial compared stem-only removal, full-tree removal, stump removal, and blading, with uncut, post-fire, and older forest references. Standardized above- and below-ground wood stakes were followed for mass loss and elemental changes, with mixed-model comparisons over time.

## Results
Harvesting slowed above-ground decay and altered or stalled below-ground decay. Increasing removal produced complex, non-additive responses, including suppression under intensive treatments and changes in nutrient retention and metal accumulation. Harvested sites did not reproduce fire-related decay dynamics.

## Discussion
Decay responses depend on treatment, soil position, element, and time, so a single endpoint can obscure important processes. The findings concern forest substrates and decomposer systems rather than direct measurement of insect nesting success.

## Conclusion
Biomass management should preserve deadwood and environmental conditions that maintain decomposition, habitat provision, and site productivity.

## Relevance to research
Indirect ecological relevance. The mockup did not measure nesting resources such as deadwood; this study supports treating substrate retention and management as potentially important habitat processes. However, a boreal forest decomposition experiment cannot establish that adding wood to green roofs benefits bees, and it tests neither bees nor rooftop richness.
